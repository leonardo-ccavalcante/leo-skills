# Action Titles — The Single Biggest Differentiator

If a reader can read only the slide titles and understand the deck, the titles are doing their job. That is the test for action titles.

## What an Action Title Is (and Isn't)

An **action title** is a complete declarative sentence that states the slide's takeaway — what the data on the slide *means* and what the reader should *conclude*.

A **topic title** is a noun phrase or label that names the subject of the slide but says nothing about what to think about it.

| Topic title (avoid) | Action title (use) |
|---|---|
| Q3 Performance | Q3 revenue beat plan by 8%, driven by enterprise upsell |
| Customer Segments | Two segments — SMB and mid-market — drive 80% of churn |
| Cost Analysis | A $12M overrun is concentrated in two vendors |
| Market Sizing | The addressable market is $4B and growing 14% annually |
| Recommendations | We recommend entering Brazil now via partnership |
| Competitive Landscape | Three competitors are consolidating; the window to act is 6 months |

The pattern: action titles have a verb and a claim. Topic titles don't.

## The Five Rules

### 1. Every title is a full sentence

If the title doesn't end with a period (or could end with one), it isn't done. "Q3 revenue grew" is a fragment; "Q3 revenue grew 12% YoY" is a sentence.

### 2. The title states the takeaway, not the topic

Ask: *what would I want the executive to walk away believing after seeing this slide?* Write that. The chart, table, or text on the slide is the *evidence* for the title — not a substitute for it.

### 3. The title is specific

"Sales improved" is vague. "Sales grew 12% YoY, beating plan by $4M" is specific. Specificity earns trust and reduces ambiguity.

### 4. The deck's titles, read in order, tell the whole story

This is the **headline test**. Read every title in sequence — top to bottom of the deck. The argument should flow: hook → evidence → recommendation. If a reader needs to read the slide bodies to follow along, the titles aren't carrying their weight.

### 5. The title matches the chart

If the title says "Costs are up in two categories" and the chart shows costs going up in five categories, one of them is wrong. Titles and exhibits must agree. If you change the chart, recheck the title.

## Anatomy of a Strong Action Title

```
[Subject]      [Verb]    [Specific claim]                    [So-what or driver]
"Churn"        "rose"    "4 points in Q3,"                   "driven by SMB onboarding"
"Two vendors"  "account" "for 80% of the cost overrun"
"The window"   "closes"  "in 6 months"                       "as competitors lock partners"
```

Most strong action titles have a subject, a verb, a specific claim, and often a driver or "so what."

## Length

Aim for one line on the slide (roughly 80–120 characters). Two lines is acceptable when the claim is genuinely complex, but three lines means the title is doing too much — split the slide or trim the title.

## Action Titles for Different Slide Types

- **Recommendation slide** — the title is the recommendation itself. *"Approve the 90-day onboarding overhaul; expected payback in 2 quarters."*
- **Chart slide** — the title states what the chart shows *and* why it matters. Not "*Revenue by quarter*" but *"Revenue growth has flattened since Q2, driven by stalled enterprise expansion.*"
- **Table slide** — the title states the takeaway from the table, not what the table is. Not *"Segment economics"* but *"SMB has the lowest CAC but also the lowest LTV; mid-market is the best blended return.*"
- **Two-column comparison** — the title states the verdict. Not *"Build vs. Buy"* but *"Buy is faster and cheaper for our use case; build only if we plan to white-label.*"
- **Section divider** — title states the section's Key Line. Not *"Section 2: Costs"* but *"Costs: a $12M overrun is concentrated in two fixable areas.*"

## Common Failure Modes

**Failure: title restates the data.** *"Revenue was $40M in Q1, $44M in Q2, $48M in Q3."* The chart already shows that. The title should say what those numbers *mean*.

**Failure: title is a question.** *"How is Q3 performing?"* Executives don't want questions; they want answers. Convert the question into the answer: *"Q3 revenue beat plan by 8%."*

**Failure: title uses jargon or hedges.** *"Performance trends suggest some upside potential."* Say what you mean: *"Q3 is on track to beat plan by $5–7M."*

**Failure: title contradicts the chart.** Always re-read the title after the chart is final.

**Failure: every title sounds the same.** *"X increased. Y increased. Z increased."* If your titles are monotonous, the deck reads as a list of facts, not an argument. Vary the verbs and let the titles build on each other.

## A Quick Self-Check

After writing all the titles, do this:

1. Read only the titles, in order, out loud.
2. Does it sound like a coherent argument that ends in a recommendation?
3. If a stranger heard only the titles, could they explain the recommendation?

If yes to all three, the deck is ready to design. If no, fix the titles — fixing them at this stage costs minutes; fixing them after slide design costs hours.
