# Storylining — Audience Fit and Editing Down

The Pyramid Principle gives you the *structure* of the argument. Storylining gives you the *cut* — the version of the argument that actually fits this audience and this meeting.

## Storylining vs. Storytelling

- **Storylining** is the outline: the specific subset of insights that lead this audience to the right action.
- **Storytelling** is the delivery: bringing that outline to life through narrative, examples, and presence.

You build the storyline before you build slides. Storytelling is what happens in the room.

## Why Storylining Matters

Most analyses produce more insight than fits in a single deck. The temptation is to include everything you found. Resist that — it weakens the argument and exhausts the audience.

The storyline forces a question: *given this audience and this meeting, what is the minimum set of insights that leads them to take the right action?*

## Five Storylining Questions

Before drafting the deck, answer these — ideally with the user. Skipping any of them is the most common cause of a deck that "doesn't land."

### 1. What decision is this meeting meant to produce?

Decks are tools for decisions. If you can't name the decision, you're not ready to draft. Examples:
- "Approve the $5M budget for the onboarding overhaul."
- "Choose between Option A and Option B for market entry."
- "Decide whether to delay the Q1 launch by 6 weeks."

If the answer is "share an update," that's a status meeting, not a decision meeting — adjust accordingly.

### 2. Who is in the audience?

Specifically:
- *What do they already know?* — don't waste slides on context they own.
- *What do they care about?* — frame the argument in terms of their priorities.
- *What will they push back on?* — anticipate it; address the strongest objection in the deck.
- *What's their risk tolerance?* — affects how aggressive your recommendation can be.

A board cares about risk-adjusted return. A product leader cares about feature trade-offs. A CFO cares about cash. Same analysis, different decks.

### 3. What is the strongest objection — and what's our answer?

Every recommendation has a credible counter. Find it before they do. Either fold the rebuttal into the deck (best) or be ready with a backup slide in the appendix.

### 4. What's the minimum evidence the audience needs to say yes?

Not the maximum you have. The minimum they need.

A useful test: imagine the audience is friendly and motivated. What's the smallest amount of analysis that gives them confidence to act? Build the main deck around that. Put the rest in the appendix.

### 5. What's the action you want them to take by the end?

Concrete and time-bound. "Approve the budget by Nov 15" — not "consider the proposal."

## How to Cut

Once you have the pyramid (Governing Thought + Key Lines + Supporting Detail), cut as follows:

1. **Drop any insight that doesn't support the Governing Thought.** Move it to the appendix or delete it entirely. It's interesting but not relevant.
2. **Drop any insight the audience already knows.** Don't spend slides telling executives things they told you last quarter.
3. **For each remaining Key Line, keep only the 2–4 strongest evidence slides.** A weaker fifth slide isn't reinforcing the argument; it's diluting it.
4. **Combine slides that make the same point.** If two slides land the same argument, pick the one with the stronger exhibit and drop the other.

## Sequencing — Where to Place the Recommendation

Two valid patterns:

### Pattern A: Recommendation up front (default for executives)

```
1. Cover
2. Executive Summary (Governing Thought as title)
3. Section: Key Line 1 + evidence
4. Section: Key Line 2 + evidence
5. Section: Key Line 3 + evidence
6. Recommendation (concrete actions)
7. Appendix
```

This is the McKinsey default and the right choice for senior audiences. The recommendation is on slide 2; everything after is the proof.

### Pattern B: Recommendation as the conclusion (occasional)

```
1. Cover
2. Context / what we set out to answer
3. Findings (Key Line 1, 2, 3)
4. Recommendation (Governing Thought)
5. Implementation
6. Appendix
```

Use this only when the recommendation is genuinely surprising or controversial and the audience needs to walk through the evidence to accept it. Most decks don't need this — start with Pattern A.

## The Three-Question Sanity Check

Before declaring the storyline done, answer:

1. *If the audience read only the Executive Summary slide, would they have what they need to decide?*
2. *If they had 5 minutes instead of 45, which 3 slides would I show?* (If you can't name them quickly, the deck doesn't have a clear backbone.)
3. *What's the one number or insight I want them to remember tomorrow?* (If everything is equally important, nothing is.)

## A Common Failure: the "We Did the Work" Deck

Some decks read like a worklog: *"Here's what we analyzed, here's the methodology, here's the data, here's the finding."* That's a process narrative, not an argument. Executives don't want to retrace your steps; they want the answer and the reasoning behind it.

The fix: lead with the conclusion. Save the methodology for the appendix.

## When Storylining Reveals Pyramid Problems

Sometimes you start cutting and realize the Governing Thought doesn't actually hold up under scrutiny — the Key Lines don't support it, or one of them collapses without the cut evidence. That's good information. Go back to the pyramid, fix it, then redo the storyline.

This iterative loop between Pyramid and Storyline is normal. Don't treat them as sequential.
